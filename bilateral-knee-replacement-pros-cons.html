Bilateral Knee Replacement: Pros and Cons

By Dr. Madhuram Chowdry
Published on August 20, 2026
8 Min Read

When arthritis affects both knees, everyday activities such as walking, climbing stairs, standing for long periods, or getting up from a chair can become increasingly difficult. If both knees have severe osteoarthritis and non-surgical treatments no longer provide enough relief, knee replacement may be considered.

Bilateral knee replacement means replacing both damaged knee joints. Depending on the patient's health, severity of arthritis, functional limitations, and surgical risk, both knees may be replaced during the same operation or through two separate surgeries.

Choosing between simultaneous and staged bilateral knee replacement is an important decision. Having both knees treated at once can mean one hospitalization and one rehabilitation period, but it also places greater physical demands on the body during a single operation. Staged surgery allows recovery from the first knee replacement before the second procedure, but it requires another operation and another recovery period.

There is no single approach that is right for everyone. The safest option depends on individual health, age, medical conditions, functional status, and the surgeon's assessment.

What Is Bilateral Knee Replacement?

Bilateral knee replacement, also called bilateral total knee arthroplasty, is a surgical procedure in which both damaged knee joints are replaced with artificial components.

It may be considered when both knees have advanced osteoarthritis that causes:

  • Persistent knee pain
  • Severe stiffness
  • Difficulty walking
  • Difficulty climbing stairs
  • Reduced mobility
  • Pain during rest or sleep
  • Significant limitation of everyday activities
  • Poor response to non-surgical treatment

Bilateral knee replacement can be performed in two main ways:

Simultaneous Bilateral Knee Replacement

Both knees are replaced during the same surgical session. This approach is sometimes called one-stage bilateral knee replacement. The potential advantage is that the patient undergoes one major operation rather than returning for a second knee replacement later. However, because both knees are operated on during the same period, the procedure can place greater demands on the patient's cardiovascular, respiratory, and overall physical reserve.

Staged Bilateral Knee Replacement

The two knees are replaced during separate operations. For example, one knee may be replaced first, followed by a period of rehabilitation before the second knee is treated. The interval between surgeries varies depending on the patient's recovery and medical circumstances. Evidence evaluating different intervals suggests that timing should be individualized rather than following one universal schedule.

Why Do Some Patients Need Both Knees Replaced?

Osteoarthritis commonly affects both knees, although the severity may differ from one knee to the other.

When arthritis becomes advanced, cartilage gradually deteriorates. This can lead to narrowing of the joint space, changes in the underlying bone, stiffness, pain, and altered alignment.

If both knees become severely affected, treating only one knee may not completely restore mobility. For example, a patient may have severe pain in both knees and find it difficult to walk even after one knee has been treated.

However, having arthritis in both knees does not automatically mean that both need to be replaced at the same time. The decision depends on symptoms, imaging findings, physical examination, overall health, and the effect of arthritis on quality of life.

Pros of Bilateral Knee Replacement

Bilateral knee replacement can offer several potential advantages, particularly for carefully selected patients.

1. Both Knees Can Be Treated at Once

The most obvious advantage of simultaneous bilateral knee replacement is that both painful knees are addressed during one surgical session. For patients with severe arthritis in both knees, this can eliminate the need to undergo a second knee replacement later.

2. One Hospitalization

Simultaneous surgery can potentially avoid a second hospital admission associated with another operation. This may be convenient for patients and families, particularly when travel, caregiving, or time away from work is an important consideration.

3. One Major Rehabilitation Period

Instead of completing rehabilitation for one knee and then repeating the process for the other, simultaneous surgery allows both knees to recover during the same overall rehabilitation period. However, rehabilitation can be demanding because both legs are recovering at the same time.

4. Balanced Treatment of Both Knees

When both knees have similar levels of severe arthritis or deformity, treating both during the same surgical episode may allow the surgeon to address both sides as part of one overall treatment plan.

5. Potentially Shorter Overall Treatment Timeline

A patient who undergoes simultaneous surgery does not need to wait several weeks or months before undergoing the second procedure. This can potentially shorten the overall journey from having two severely arthritic knees to having both knees treated.

6. One Anesthesia and Surgical Episode

Simultaneous bilateral surgery means the patient does not need a second separate anesthesia and surgical episode. However, this advantage must be balanced against the fact that the single operation involves treatment of both knees and may place greater demands on the body.

Cons of Bilateral Knee Replacement

The advantages of simultaneous bilateral knee replacement need to be considered alongside potential disadvantages.

1. Greater Physical Stress on the Body

Replacing both knees during one operation is a major procedure. The body must recover from surgery involving both lower limbs at the same time. This is one reason patient selection is extremely important. The AAOS states that simultaneous bilateral knee replacement may be considered in appropriately selected patients but should be performed cautiously and avoided in patients at high risk of cardiopulmonary complications.

2. Higher Risk in Some Patients

Research comparing simultaneous and staged bilateral knee replacement has found different complication profiles. A 2026 systematic review of more than 570,000 patients reported higher observed short-term mortality, deep-vein thrombosis, and transfusion requirements with simultaneous bilateral surgery, while staged surgery showed higher rates of some infections. The researchers emphasized that neither approach is universally superior and that patient selection is important. These findings do not mean that simultaneous surgery is unsafe for every patient. They highlight why the procedure should be considered only after a detailed medical and surgical assessment.

3. Rehabilitation Can Be More Challenging

After unilateral knee replacement, the opposite leg can provide support during walking and rehabilitation. After simultaneous bilateral surgery, both knees are recovering. This can make standing up, walking, using stairs, getting in and out of bed, and performing exercises more challenging during the early recovery period. Patients may require additional assistance initially.

4. Greater Blood Loss and Transfusion Risk

Bilateral surgery can involve greater blood loss than a single-knee procedure. Recent evidence has found higher transfusion requirements in some analyses of simultaneous bilateral knee replacement compared with staged surgery. Modern surgical and blood-management techniques can help reduce these risks, but the patient's medical history still needs to be considered.

5. Greater Concern About Blood Clots

Major lower-limb surgery can increase the risk of venous thromboembolism. Some systematic reviews have found higher rates of deep-vein thrombosis and/or pulmonary embolism with simultaneous bilateral knee replacement compared with staged procedures. Doctors use measures such as early mobilization, appropriate medication, compression strategies, and individualized risk assessment to reduce this risk.

6. Not Suitable for Everyone

Patients with significant heart, lung, or other medical problems may not be suitable candidates for simultaneous bilateral surgery. Age alone does not determine suitability. Instead, the surgical team considers overall health, medical conditions, fitness, medications, previous medical history, and the patient's ability to tolerate a major operation.

Simultaneous vs Staged Bilateral Knee Replacement

Feature Simultaneous Surgery Staged Surgery
Number of operations One Two
Hospitalization Usually one surgical admission Separate admissions may be required
Rehabilitation Both knees recover together One knee recovers before the second
Overall treatment timeline Potentially shorter Usually longer
Second anesthesia Not required for the second knee Required for second procedure
Early mobility Both legs recovering simultaneously One operated knee with the other leg available for support
Patient selection Requires careful medical assessment May be suitable for a wider range of patients
Surgical risk Greater physiological demand in one episode Risk distributed across two procedures

The evidence comparing the two approaches is mixed and has evolved over time. Some studies report similar overall complication rates among carefully matched patients, while multiple meta-analyses have identified increased risks of certain complications with simultaneous surgery. Therefore, the decision should not be based simply on which procedure appears faster or more convenient.

Who May Be a Candidate for Simultaneous Bilateral Knee Replacement?

There is no single checklist that guarantees suitability. A patient may be considered for simultaneous surgery when:

  • Both knees have significant arthritis
  • Both knees cause substantial symptoms
  • Non-surgical treatment has not provided sufficient relief
  • The patient is medically fit for major surgery
  • Cardiovascular and respiratory risks are acceptable
  • The patient understands the rehabilitation requirements
  • The surgical team considers the overall risk acceptable

The AAOS specifically recommends caution and avoidance of simultaneous bilateral TKA in patients who are at high risk of cardiopulmonary complications. A comprehensive preoperative assessment is therefore essential.

Who May Benefit From Staged Bilateral Knee Replacement?

Staged surgery may be considered when the surgical team believes that treating one knee at a time provides a safer or more manageable approach. It may be particularly relevant when:

  • The patient has significant medical conditions
  • The patient may not tolerate a long or physiologically demanding operation
  • One knee is considerably worse than the other
  • The patient wants to recover from one operation before undergoing another
  • Rehabilitation with both knees simultaneously would be difficult
  • The surgeon recommends separating the procedures based on individual risk

Staged surgery also allows the patient and surgical team to evaluate recovery from the first knee before planning the second procedure.

How Long Should You Wait Between Two Knee Replacements?

There is no universally appropriate interval for every patient. The timing of the second knee replacement depends on:

  • Recovery from the first surgery
  • Muscle strength
  • Walking ability
  • Wound healing
  • Medical stability
  • Pain control
  • Rehabilitation progress
  • Overall health

A 2025 network meta-analysis found that, among the observational studies included, staged surgery at intervals of more than six weeks and less than six months was associated with lower mortality and complication risk than simultaneous surgery. However, the researchers also noted limitations associated with non-randomized evidence.

The interval should therefore be decided individually rather than using a fixed rule.

Recovery After Bilateral Knee Replacement

Recovery after bilateral knee replacement varies from person to person. With simultaneous surgery, both knees begin recovery at approximately the same time.

Early rehabilitation generally focuses on:

  • Pain control
  • Knee movement
  • Muscle activation
  • Safe transfers
  • Walking
  • Strengthening exercises
  • Preventing complications
  • Gradually increasing independence

With staged surgery, rehabilitation begins with the first knee while the other knee remains available to provide support. Once the patient has recovered sufficiently, the second knee can be addressed.

The exact recovery timeline depends on age, muscle strength, overall health, surgical technique, rehabilitation participation, and other individual factors.

Is Bilateral Knee Replacement Better Than One Knee at a Time?

There is no universally better option.

For a healthy, carefully selected patient with severe arthritis in both knees, simultaneous bilateral knee replacement may offer the convenience of treating both knees in one surgical episode.

For another patient, staged surgery may provide a safer and more manageable recovery.

Recent research supports this individualized approach. A large 2026 meta-analysis concluded that simultaneous and staged bilateral knee replacement have distinct risk profiles rather than one approach being universally superior.

The goal should not simply be to complete both operations as quickly as possible. The goal is to choose the approach that provides the best balance of safety, pain relief, mobility, recovery, and long-term function for the individual patient.

Role of Robotic-Assisted Knee Replacement

Robotic-assisted technology may be used in knee replacement surgery for selected patients. The technology can assist the surgical team with preoperative planning, bone preparation, alignment assessment, and implant positioning.

It is important to understand that a robotic system does not independently perform the operation. The orthopaedic surgeon remains responsible for the surgical plan and procedure.

What Questions Should You Ask Your Orthopaedic Surgeon?

Before deciding between simultaneous and staged bilateral knee replacement, consider asking:

  • Do both of my knees require replacement?
  • Am I medically suitable for simultaneous bilateral surgery?
  • Would staged surgery be safer for me?
  • What are my individual risks?
  • How long would you expect my rehabilitation to take?
  • What support would I need at home?
  • How long should I wait before considering the second knee?
  • What complications should I watch for?
  • Would robotic-assisted surgery be appropriate for my condition?
  • What can I expect regarding pain relief and mobility after surgery?

These questions can help you have a more informed discussion with your orthopaedic surgeon.

Can Both Knees Be Replaced at the Same Time?

Yes, both knees can be replaced during the same surgical episode in appropriately selected patients.

However, simultaneous bilateral knee replacement is not automatically the best choice simply because it reduces the number of surgical episodes.

The potential benefits of one-stage surgery need to be weighed against the increased physical demands and possible risks.

The AAOS recommends that simultaneous bilateral TKA be considered cautiously and only in appropriately selected patients, particularly avoiding it in those at high cardiopulmonary risk.

Conclusion

Bilateral knee replacement can be an effective treatment for patients whose both knees have severe arthritis and whose pain and disability are no longer adequately controlled with non-surgical treatment.

The major advantage of simultaneous bilateral knee replacement is convenience: both knees can be treated during one surgical episode, potentially reducing the need for a second hospitalization and separate rehabilitation period.

However, simultaneous surgery also places greater physical demands on the patient. Research has identified increased risks of certain complications, including blood clots, transfusion, and mortality in some study populations, while staged surgery has its own disadvantages and may involve additional hospitalization, anesthesia, cost, and recovery time.

For this reason, there is no universal answer to whether simultaneous or staged bilateral knee replacement is better.

The right decision depends on your knee condition, medical history, cardiovascular and respiratory health, mobility, age, fitness, and ability to tolerate and recover from major surgery.

A detailed evaluation by an experienced orthopaedic surgeon can help determine whether bilateral knee replacement is appropriate and whether simultaneous or staged surgery is the safer option.
